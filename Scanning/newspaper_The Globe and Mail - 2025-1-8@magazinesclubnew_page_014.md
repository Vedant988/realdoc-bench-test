There are many worthwhile conventions that enable a good defensive pair to score all the tricks to which they're entitled. Some players ignore these conventions, relying more on instinct than science, but this is a dangerous way to play the game.
Like the present deal, which features top-notch partnership defence, West leads a club, the ace. East, diagnosing the lead as a possible suit, returns the three of clubs, ruffing West with the deuce.
East's return of the three, rather than just any club, is conventional. It says that, between hearts and diamonds, he would prefer that West lead the lower-ranking suit next. (Had East preferred a heart return, he would have lost the right of clubs to the higher-ranking suit.)
Accordingly, West leads the diamond three to East's queen and South's ace. Declarer plays a trump to dummy's jack and East's ace, and now fast has a genuine problem.
If he thinks West has another trump, East should return a club, but if he thinks West has no more trump, East's best chance is a diamond return. With only his own suit going, West will probably be the only one to play the diamond contract. But another convention, the trump echo, comes to his rescue.
West's ruff with the two of spades, followed later by the play of the four, denotes a bidding of three trumps. If West had started with the ♠aq, for ex ample, he would have ruffed the club at trick two with the four and later played the deuce to indicate possession of a third trump.
East therefore returns a diamond at trick five, and four spades goes down two. With a club return, South makes the contract.
